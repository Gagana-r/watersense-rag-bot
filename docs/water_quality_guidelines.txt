WATER QUALITY GUIDELINES AND PARAMETERS
WHO Guidelines for Drinking Water Quality

1. pH LEVELS
The acceptable pH range for drinking water is 6.5 to 8.5.
Water with pH below 6.5 is considered acidic and can corrode pipes.
Water with pH above 8.5 is considered alkaline and may cause scaling.
The ideal pH for drinking water is between 7.0 and 7.5.

2. TURBIDITY
Turbidity measures the cloudiness of water caused by suspended particles.
WHO guideline for turbidity is less than 1 NTU (Nephelometric Turbidity Unit).
High turbidity indicates presence of sediments, bacteria, or other contaminants.
Turbidity is measured using a turbidimeter or nephelometer.

3. DISSOLVED OXYGEN (DO)
Dissolved oxygen is essential for aquatic life.
Acceptable DO levels for drinking water: 6-8 mg/L.
Low DO levels indicate organic pollution or biological contamination.
DO below 4 mg/L is harmful to aquatic organisms.

4. NITRATES
WHO guideline for nitrates in drinking water: 50 mg/L maximum.
High nitrate levels cause methemoglobinemia (blue baby syndrome) in infants.
Nitrates enter water from agricultural fertilizers and sewage.
Safe limit for infants: less than 10 mg/L nitrate-nitrogen.

5. HEAVY METALS
Lead: Maximum 0.01 mg/L (WHO guideline)
Arsenic: Maximum 0.01 mg/L
Mercury: Maximum 0.006 mg/L
Cadmium: Maximum 0.003 mg/L
Chromium: Maximum 0.05 mg/L
Heavy metals cause serious health effects including kidney damage, neurological disorders.

6. TOTAL DISSOLVED SOLIDS (TDS)
Acceptable TDS range: 500 mg/L (WHO guideline), 1000 mg/L maximum.
TDS measures all dissolved minerals, salts, and metals in water.
High TDS gives water a salty or bitter taste.
Low TDS water may be corrosive.

7. COLIFORM BACTERIA
Total coliform bacteria should be 0 per 100 mL in drinking water.
E. coli presence indicates fecal contamination.
Coliform testing is the primary indicator of water safety.
Presence of coliforms requires immediate treatment.

8. WATER TREATMENT METHODS
Chlorination: Most common disinfection method, kills bacteria and viruses.
Filtration: Removes suspended particles, sediments, and some microorganisms.
Reverse Osmosis (RO): Removes dissolved solids, heavy metals, and contaminants.
UV Treatment: Kills microorganisms without adding chemicals.
Boiling: Kills pathogens but does not remove chemical contaminants.
Coagulation and Flocculation: Removes turbidity and suspended particles.

9. WATER QUALITY TESTING PARAMETERS
Physical parameters: Color, odor, taste, turbidity, temperature
Chemical parameters: pH, TDS, hardness, alkalinity, dissolved oxygen
Biological parameters: Total coliform, E. coli, fecal coliform
Radiological parameters: Radioactive substances

10. HEALTH EFFECTS OF CONTAMINATED WATER
Cholera: Caused by Vibrio cholerae bacteria in contaminated water.
Typhoid: Caused by Salmonella typhi from fecal contamination.
Dysentery: Caused by Shigella bacteria or Entamoeba histolytica.
Fluorosis: Caused by excess fluoride (above 1.5 mg/L).
Arsenicosis: Caused by long-term exposure to arsenic in groundwater.
Kidney damage: Caused by heavy metals like lead, cadmium, mercury.

11. WATER HARDNESS
Soft water: 0-60 mg/L as CaCO3
Moderately hard: 61-120 mg/L as CaCO3
Hard water: 121-180 mg/L as CaCO3
Very hard water: above 180 mg/L as CaCO3
Hard water causes scaling in pipes and reduces soap lathering.

12. FLUORIDE
WHO guideline for fluoride: 1.5 mg/L maximum.
Optimal fluoride for dental health: 0.5-1.0 mg/L.
Excess fluoride causes dental and skeletal fluorosis.
Deficiency of fluoride increases risk of dental cavities.

13. CHLORINE RESIDUAL
Free chlorine residual in distribution system: 0.2-0.5 mg/L.
Maximum chlorine residual: 5 mg/L.
Chlorine residual prevents recontamination during distribution.
Excess chlorine causes taste and odor problems.

14. TEMPERATURE
Optimal temperature for drinking water: 10-15 degrees Celsius.
Higher temperatures promote bacterial growth.
Temperature affects taste, odor, and chemical reactions in water.

15. WATER QUALITY MONITORING
Regular monitoring is essential for safe drinking water supply.
Sampling frequency depends on population size and source type.
Parameters should be tested at source, treatment plant, and distribution points.
Results should be compared against WHO or national standards.
